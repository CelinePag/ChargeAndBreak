\begin{abstract}
Battery-electric trucks must schedule charging jointly with the rest and break
periods that driving-hours regulation imposes, under stochastic travel times.
A rolling-horizon look-ahead that solves its tail exactly is the strongest
implementable policy we have for this problem, but it costs 45--90\,s per
decision and up to four hours of compute per route: a \emph{batch} policy, not
an operational one. We ask how much of it survives amortisation. Cloning it
into a 36k-parameter masked network yields a policy that decides in
\textbf{0.74\,ms} --- roughly $10^5\times$ faster --- and lands
\textbf{$+0.43\pm0.18\%$} of realised route duration behind its own teacher on
held-out instances, against $+2.74\%$ for a rule-based heuristic of comparable
speed. The route there is not automatic: our design decisions were repeatedly
made against \emph{closed-loop} evidence that contradicted the supervised
metrics, and the change that mattered most was a class-weighting choice that
raised balanced accuracy from 0.299 to 0.477 while making realised duration
six times worse. We also report a negative result: DAgger improved the
development set but did not transfer to the held-out set. We argue that in
sequential operational settings, offline predictive accuracy is not merely a
weak proxy for policy quality but is frequently \emph{anti-correlated} with it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem and motivation}
\label{sec:intro}

A battery-electric heavy truck on a long corridor faces two coupled schedules.
Charging is dictated by energy and by where stations are; rest and break
periods are dictated by EU driving-hours regulation~\citep{eu561}, on a
corridor whose station spacing is itself set by
regulation~\citep{eu2023afir}. The two schedules
interact: a mandatory break taken \emph{inside} a charging dwell is free,
because the two run in parallel, so \emph{when} to charge is inseparable from
\emph{when} to rest. Travel times are stochastic, so the schedule must be
decided online, stop by stop.

Our companion study benchmarks a family of policies on this problem --- a
myopic rule-based heuristic, static robust plans, a two-stage stochastic plan
with recourse, a rolling-horizon look-ahead, and a per-realisation hindsight
oracle. Here we take that benchmark as given and ask a narrower question about
the best implementable member of it.

\paragraph{Where the remaining headroom is.}
A one-at-a-time sweep of the look-ahead's parameters is unusually
uninformative in two of its three dimensions. Raising the scenario count from
10 to 50 moves realised duration by at most $0.4\%$; extending the horizon
beyond one duty cycle (24\,h) moves it by at most $0.2\%$, although
\emph{shortening} it below one duty cycle costs up to $1.7\%$. The one live
axis is \textbf{integrality}: solving the look-ahead tail as a MIP rather than
an LP relaxation roughly halves the gap to the hindsight optimum
(Table~\ref{tab:motivation}), and costs an order of magnitude more time per
decision.

\paragraph{The gap this paper attacks.}
Exactness is therefore worth about two points of optimality and is now our
standard configuration --- but at 45--90\,s per decision (median, by route
class) and 0.6--4.1\,h of wall clock per route it is affordable only as an
offline campaign. It cannot be re-evaluated continuously in a cab, it cannot be
run across a fleet by a dispatcher, and it requires a commercial solver licence
at the point of use. We therefore ask: \emph{how much of an exact look-ahead
survives being compressed into a policy that decides in milliseconds?} In
Powell's taxonomy~\citep{powell2022rlso} this converts a direct look-ahead into
a policy-function approximation; in the ML-for-combinatorial-optimisation
framing~\citep{bengio2021ml4co} it is amortised
optimisation~\citep{dumouchelle2022neur2sp,nair2020neuraldiving}. Unlike
neural construction heuristics for routing~\citep{kool2019attention} the
sequence of stops here is fixed and the learned object is the operational
schedule; the closest application is safe reinforcement learning for dynamic
electric vehicle routing~\citep{basso2022dsevrp}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Decision process.}
At stop $i$ the state comprises the vehicle's state of charge, the three
regulatory clocks (consecutive driving, shift driving, shift work), the
split-break flag, the reduced-rest and extended-shift budgets consumed, and the
arrival time; plus a description of the road ahead --- the next $K$ nodes (leg
duration and energy, whether the node is a charger or a customer, queue delay,
and time-window slack \emph{relative to now}) and aggregates over the whole
remaining route. With $K=20$ this is 141 features. The action is the joint
discrete choice
$a_i \in \{\text{charge},\lnot\text{charge}\}\times\{\text{none},b_{15},b_{30},b_{45}\}
\times\{\text{none},r_1,r_2\}$, of which 12 combinations occur in the data,
together with a charge duration $\tau_c$. The reward is the negative increment
of the benchmark's own objective, so training and evaluation optimise the same
quantity.

\paragraph{Policy.}
A two-layer MLP ($141\to128\to128$; 36{,}365 parameters) with two heads: masked
logits over the 12 actions, and a softplus charge duration. Two constraint
layers wrap it, both reusing the benchmark's existing feasibility checks, so
the learned policy is subject to exactly the rules every other policy obeys.
\emph{Masking} sets structurally impossible actions to $-\infty$ before the
softmax (no charging away from a station; no reduced rest without budget).
\emph{Forcing} restricts the choice set further when a one-step check declares
a charge, break or rest mandatory --- the network still chooses freely
\emph{among} compliant actions, so the rules constrain rather than decide.
Charge duration is clamped below by the energy needed to reach the next station
at the 0.5-quantile of consumption, the same rule the teacher uses to size its
own committed charge. No solver runs at deployment.

\paragraph{Data.}
767 exact-tail look-ahead runs over a $3\times3\times4$ grid of route length,
customer density and time-window class supply \textbf{74{,}380} labelled
decisions; the simulator records the per-stop state, so no replay is needed.
Instances are split by seed into 530 train / 129 validation / 108 test. Every
design decision below was made on validation; the test set was evaluated once.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

Every policy is executed in the same simulator, over the same recorded
travel-time realisations, and scored by the same metrics. A run that breaches a
regulatory limit \emph{halts} at that stop, so a route duration exists only for
a completed route: halted runs are counted, never averaged into a duration. We
report the paired difference in realised duration against the exact-tail
teacher on the same instance, over three independently trained seeds.

\paragraph{Main result.}
Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:money} give the headline. The cloned
policy sits $0.43\%$ behind a teacher that takes 70\,s per decision, while
deciding in $0.74$\,ms and requiring no solver; the rule-based heuristic, which
is equally fast, is six times further behind. The student halts on 2.7 of 108
routes against the teacher's zero --- the residual cost of amortisation. Those
failures are not a diffuse loss of quality: every one of them is a breach of
the 15\,h daily-spread ceiling.

\paragraph{Behavioural fidelity.}
Aggregate numbers hide whether the policy has learned the \emph{structure} of
the problem or merely its cost. Figure~\ref{fig:schedule} shows one realised
schedule against the teacher's: rest placement, charging episodes and the
state-of-charge trajectory are essentially superimposed, and both policies hide
almost every mandatory break inside a charging dwell --- the coupling that makes
this problem non-separable. Quantitatively the student charges for $6.74$\,h per
route against the teacher's $6.80$\,h ($-0.9\%$) and runs the pack to the same
depth (5th-percentile state of charge $29.5\%$ against $28.2\%$): it has learned
that charge is time, not merely that empty batteries are bad.

\paragraph{Ablations, decided in closed loop.}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} pairs each design decision's supervised metric with its
realised effect. The pattern is consistent, and is the most useful thing we
have to report: \emph{the supervised metric moves the wrong way}.
Inverse-frequency class weighting is the clearest case. The decision
distribution is dominated by ``keep driving'' ($87.9\%$), so weighting rare
classes up is the textbook response, and by its own measure it works ---
balanced accuracy rises from $0.299$ to $0.477$. But it places the majority
class at weight $0.035$ against $5.3$ for the rarest, so predicting a
\emph{rest} when the truth is \emph{drive on} becomes nearly free in the loss,
while costing 9--11\,h of route time in reality. Realised duration degrades from
$+0.56\%$ to $+3.17\%$. The loss had quietly decorrelated from the objective,
and no offline metric revealed it.

\paragraph{A negative result: DAgger did not transfer.}
Behaviour cloning trains on the teacher's state distribution, and a policy that
acts creates its own; DAgger~\citep{ross2011dagger} is the standard remedy. We
ran one round: the student drove 530 training instances while the exact-tail
teacher labelled a sampled $35\%$ of the states it reached, yielding
\textbf{17{,}941} aggregated labels ($19.4\%$ of the training set). The
distribution shift is real and measurable --- at student-visited states the
teacher prescribes rest markedly more often than in the base data ($r_1$:
$0.33\%\to0.81\%$; $r_2$: $1.63\%\to2.33\%$), consistent with a policy that
arrives at stops with its clocks closer to their limits. On validation the
aggregated model looked better (median $+0.19\%$ against $+0.44\%$; halts $3.3$
against $5.7$). \emph{On the held-out set the effect reversed}
(Table~\ref{tab:main}): $+0.48\%$ against $+0.43\%$, with more halts rather than
fewer. The validation gain was within twice the seed-to-seed spread and did not
survive. We suspect a single DAgger round is the wrong tool once the state
distribution has already been repaired by fixing the loss.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}

An exact-tail look-ahead can be compressed into a 36k-parameter network that
decides ${\sim}10^5\times$ faster and gives up about half a percent of route
duration. That is the difference between a policy that can be run as an offline
study and one that can be run in a vehicle.

The transferable lesson is methodological. Repeatedly an offline metric and a
closed-loop measurement disagreed, and each time the closed-loop measurement was
the one worth believing; with class weighting, following standard practice would
have made the deployed policy six times worse. In sequential operational problems
the cost of an error is wildly non-uniform across the action space --- a
misplaced daily rest costs nine hours, a misplaced charging decision costs
minutes --- and any offline loss that treats them comparably has stopped
measuring what matters. We would encourage reporting realised closed-loop cost as
the primary metric for learned policies in operations, with predictive accuracy
relegated to a diagnostic.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Single vehicle; synthetic travel-time uncertainty from a fitted shifted
lognormal; imitation bounds the policy by its teacher by construction; three
training seeds. The residual failures concentrate in one regulatory constraint
(the daily spread ceiling), which suggests a targeted feasibility layer rather
than more data. Extending to a fleet sharing charging infrastructure is the
natural next step, and turns a single-agent problem into a multi-agent one.
