\title{Improving on a Stochastic Look-Ahead: Rollout and Expert Iteration
for Scheduling Electric Truck Drivers under Uncertainty}
\titlerunning{Improving on a Stochastic Look-Ahead}

\author{C\'eline Pagnier\inst{1}\orcidID{0009-0009-7276-9525} \and
Steffen J. S. Bakker\inst{2,3}\orcidID{0000-0002-2656-3827}}
\authorrunning{C. Pagnier and S. J. S. Bakker}
\institute{Department of Industrial Economics and Technology Management,
Norwegian University of Science and Technology, Trondheim, Norway}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Battery electric trucks can match diesel route durations on long-haul routes
if charging is synchronized with the breaks and rests imposed by the EU
Hours-of-Service regulation, but uncertain travel times make this
synchronization fragile. A rolling-horizon look-ahead policy (LA), which
solves a mixed-integer linear program for every candidate action and
travel-time scenario, performed best in our previous work, but its scores are
biased by the perfect information within each scenario and by its 24-hour
horizon. This paper shows that policies learned from the LA can improve on it.
A neural network and gradient-boosted trees learn the costs that the LA
computes for every candidate action, behind a rule-based safety filter. Where
the learned policy hesitates, a rollout simulates its best candidate actions
to the destination, and expert iteration trains the network on these
simulations. On 125 test routes, the rollout reduces the gap to a
perfect-hindsight oracle from 3.05\% for the LA to 2.38\% without calling a
solver, and to 1.84\% after expert iteration.
\keywords{Rollout algorithms \and Expert iteration \and Imitation learning
\and Electric trucks \and Truck driver scheduling}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Battery electric trucks are the leading pathway to decarbonize road
freight~\cite{pagnier2026linking}, but on long-haul routes their charging
stops interact with the EU Hours-of-Service (HoS) regulation~\cite{eu561},
which imposes a 45-minute break after at most 4.5 hours of driving and a daily
rest within every 24 hours. Because such a break is long enough for a
substantial fast charge, synchronizing charging with breaks and rests keeps
electric trucks close to diesel route durations~\cite{brockmann2025}. However,
this synchronization is fragile under uncertain travel times.

This paper is a continuation of~\cite{pagnier2026scheduling}, in which we
formulated this problem as a sequential decision model and compared several
policies in a discrete-event simulator. The best one is a rolling-horizon
look-ahead (LA), which solves a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) over the
next 24 hours for every candidate action and 25 travel-time scenarios. It
stays within 2 to 5\% of a perfect-hindsight oracle, but it needs more than a
minute of solver time per decision, and its scores are biased, since each
scenario is solved with perfect information and what happens after 24 hours
is not counted. A policy that imitates the LA inherits these biases. However,
a learned policy decides in milliseconds, so that it can be simulated to the
destination, which evaluates a decision over the whole route without
knowledge of the future. This motivates our research questions: Can a policy
learned from the LA be used to improve on the LA? And can this improvement be
learned back into a fast policy?

Our contributions are as follows. First, we train a policy on the costs that
the LA computes for all candidate actions and execute it behind a rule-based
safety filter. Second, we propose a decisive-state rollout, which simulates
the best candidate actions of the learned policy where it hesitates, without
calling a solver. Third, we train the learned policy on the evaluations of
this rollout, in the manner of expert iteration~\cite{anthony2017}. Finally,
we evaluate these policies on routes whose charging stations (CS) have equal
and different powers.

Our results show that the rollout improves on the LA at about 1\% of its
computing time, and that after expert iteration the network alone matches the
LA. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Sections~\ref{sec:related} to~\ref{sec:method} review related work, the
problem and the policies, Sect.~\ref{sec:exp} reports the results, and
Sect.~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Our work combines three research areas, which we review in turn before
positioning our contribution.

\subsubsection{Learning from optimization experts.}
DAgger~\cite{ross2011} trains a policy on states that the learned policy
visits and an expert labels, and
AggreVaTe~\cite{ross_bagnell2014,sun2017aggrevated} learns from the expert's
cost-to-go of every action, which bounds the cost of the learned policy by
that of the expert plus the regression error. The LA is an instance of the
expectation algorithm~\cite{vanhentenryck2006} and of hindsight
optimization~\cite{yoon2008}, whose estimates are optimistic, as in an
information relaxation~\cite{brown2010}, and policies can also be learned
from hindsight-optimal solutions~\cite{baty2024,sinclair2023}. Learned
controllers are often executed behind a safety filter~\cite{wabersich2021} or
a shield~\cite{alshiekh2018}. At CPAIOR, reinforcement learning has been used
to fine-tune policies that imitate a global solver~\cite{bourgeat2026} or
solve a MILP~\cite{anand2026}.

\subsubsection{Rollout and policy improvement.}
Rollout algorithms improve a base policy by simulating it after each candidate
action~\cite{tesauro1996,bertsekas1997}. This is one step of policy iteration,
so that the rollout policy is no worse than its base policy when the
simulations are exact. Rollout is widely used in stochastic dynamic vehicle
routing~\cite{goodson2017,kullman2021}, and approximate policy iteration
learns the improved policy from rollout estimates~\cite{lagoudakis2003}.
Expert iteration~\cite{anthony2017} alternates between a search that improves
on a neural network and the training of the network on the decisions of the
search, and Chang et al.~\cite{chang2015lols} argue that improving on an
expert requires roll-outs with the learned policy.

\subsubsection{Electric trucks and driving-time regulations.}
Truck driver scheduling under EU rules~\cite{goel2010,garaix2024} and electric
vehicle routing with recharging stations~\cite{schneider2014,montoya2017} have
mostly been studied separately. Recent works combine them on a fixed route
with deterministic travel times~\cite{bai2023,brockmann2025}, the first with a
rollout algorithm, and dynamic electric vehicle routing has been addressed
with rollout~\cite{kullman2021} or safe reinforcement
learning~\cite{basso2022}, but without driving-time rules.

\subsubsection{Contribution.}
Table~\ref{tab:positioning} positions the present work against the most
closely related papers. To our knowledge, learned policies have not yet been
applied to the joint charging and rest decisions of electric trucks, nor used
as the base policy of a rollout that improves on the look-ahead they are
learned from.

\section{Problem and Look-Ahead Policy}
\label{sec:problem}

We briefly recall the sequential decision model
of~\cite{pagnier2026scheduling}, which follows the framework of
Powell~\cite{powell2019}.

\subsubsection{Route, uncertainty and decisions.}
A truck drives a fixed route of stops $i\in\mathcal{I}=\{0,\dots,N\}$:
customers $\mathcal{C}$, CS $\mathcal{K}$ and rest areas $\mathcal{L}$. Leg $i$
has an average travel time $D_i$ and a realized travel time $\tilde
D_i=D_i\,\xi_i$, where $\xi_i=\min\{\underline{\xi}+Y_i,\ \overline{\xi}\}$
has mean 1 and a coefficient of variation of 0.15, with
$\underline{\xi}\approx0.889$, $\overline{\xi}=1.6$ and $Y_i$ lognormal and
independent across legs. The energy of a leg follows from its realized
speed. The state $\Gamma_i$ at stop $i$
contains the clock time, the battery energy and the counters of the HoS
regulation, and an action $a_i$ combines a charging decision, a break (45
minutes, or 15 and 30 minutes) and a daily rest (11 or 9 hours) with their
durations; $\mathcal{A}_i$ is the set of actions that are structurally
feasible at stop $i$. A driver may drive at most 4.5 hours without a break and
9 hours per shift (10 hours twice a week), and must complete a daily rest
within 24 hours of the previous one~\cite{eu561,eu2002_15}. A charge counts
as a break if it lasts as long as the break. The 500\,kWh battery must stay above
20\% of its capacity, and charging slows down above 80\%. A run in which the
truck is stranded or the driver breaches a limit is infeasible. Each customer
has a service time and a soft time window, and we seek a policy $\pi$,
$a_i=A^\pi(\Gamma_i)$, that minimizes
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\Big[u^t_N+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}\beta_i\delta_i\Big],
\label{eq:obj}
\end{equation}
the expected arrival time at the destination plus a penalty $\beta_i$ of 30
minutes for each customer served outside its window ($\delta_i=1$).

\subsubsection{Look-ahead policy and oracle.}
At stop $i$, the LA draws $S=25$ travel-time scenarios $\xi^{(k)}$ and solves,
for every $a\in\mathcal{A}_i$ and every $k$, the MILP
of~\cite{pagnier2026scheduling} up to a horizon stop $h$ within 24 hours,
with $a$ fixed at stop $i$. It executes the action with the lowest score
$f_i(a)=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{k=1}^{S}z_i(a,\xi^{(k)})$, where $z_i(a,\xi^{(k)})$
is the optimal arrival time at $h$ plus penalties, or a large constant if the
scenario is infeasible. The oracle solves the MILP over the whole route with
the realized travel times, and we report the gap of each policy to it.

This score is biased in two ways. First, each scenario is solved with perfect
information, so that $f_i(a)$ is optimistic~\cite{brown2010}, the more so
after actions that leave little slack to the limits. Second, the end of the
horizon is valued only by the arrival time at $h$, so that a daily rest needed
after $h$ is not seen. In contrast, simulating a policy to the destination
measures the cost of an action over the whole route, without knowledge of the
future.

\section{Learned, Rollout and Expert-Iteration Policies}
\label{sec:method}

\subsubsection{Learning from the action costs of the LA.}
The LA computes $f_i(a)$ for every $a\in\mathcal{A}_i$, and we record all
these scores rather than its choice only. For the actions
$\mathcal{A}^F_i$ that are feasible in all scenarios, the regression target is
the regret $q_i(a)=f_i(a)-\min_{a'\in\mathcal{A}^F_i}f_i(a')$, and
$\mathbf{1}\{a\in\mathcal{A}^F_i\}$ is the target of a feasibility model. A
state--action pair is described by 95 features at average travel times, such
as the slack to each regulatory limit, the energy margin to the next CS and
the next customers. A regret model $\hat q$, a feasibility model $\hat p$ and a
charging-duration model are trained as gradient-boosted trees
(LightGBM~\cite{ke2017}) or as multilayer perceptrons
(PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019}), with a Huber loss on the regret, to which the
network adds a cross-entropy between the softmax rankings
$\mathrm{sm}(-q_i/\tau)$ and $\mathrm{sm}(-\hat q_i/\tau)$, with
$\tau=0.25$\,h.

\subsubsection{Safety filter.}
The learned policy $\hat\pi$ selects $\hat a_i\in\arg\min\{\hat q_i(a):
a\in\hat{\mathcal{A}}_i,\ \hat p_i(a)\ge0.5\}$, where the set
$\hat{\mathcal{A}}_i\subseteq\mathcal{A}_i$ is computed by rules: it removes
an action if a regulatory counter would exceed its limit when the next leg is
driven at the 0.99 quantile of $\xi_i$, if the stop would not fit before the
next daily rest, or if another action is mandatory. The charge is clipped
between the energy needed to reach the next CS at the same quantile and a full
battery.

\subsubsection{Decisive-state rollout.}
Let $Q^{\hat\pi}_i(a)$ be the expected cost~(\ref{eq:obj}) when $a$ is
applied at stop $i$ and $\hat\pi$ takes all subsequent decisions. A policy that
applies $\arg\min_{a\in C_i}Q^{\hat\pi}_i(a)$ at every stop, for candidate sets
$C_i\ni\hat a_i$, is no worse than $\hat\pi$~\cite{bertsekas1997}. We estimate
$Q^{\hat\pi}$ by simulation, only at \emph{decisive} states, where the best
rest has a predicted regret within $c=2$ hours of the best action without
rest, or the two best actions are within $m=0.25$ hour. There, the candidates
are the $K=4$ actions of $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_i$ with the lowest $\hat q_i$,
including the best rest and the best action without rest. Each candidate is
simulated with $\hat\pi$ to the destination under $F=32$ travel-time futures
shared by all candidates~\cite{ng2000pegasus}, so that the cost differences
$d_{jk}$ between candidate $j$ and $\hat a_i$ in future $k$ are paired, and an
infeasible simulated run is charged the average cost of $\hat a_i$ plus 24
hours. The futures are evaluated in rounds of 8, after which a candidate with
$\bar d_j-2\,\mathrm{se}(d_j)>0$ is dropped, and the policy applies the
remaining candidate with the lowest $\bar d_j$ if it is negative, and $\hat
a_i$ otherwise. Since every candidate belongs to $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_i$ and
every simulated decision is taken by $\hat\pi$, the safety filter applies
unchanged. These parameters were fixed before any validation run.

\subsubsection{Expert iteration.}
Expert iteration~\cite{anthony2017} transfers the improvement of the rollout
to the network. We run $\hat\pi$ on the training routes under fresh
travel-time draws and, at every decisive state, evaluate its candidates as
above while executing $\hat a_i$, so that the labeled states follow the
distribution of the learned policy~\cite{ross2011}. A labeled state $s$ gives
the candidates $C_s$ and their measured advantages $\bar d_{sj}$, with $\bar
d_{sj}=0$ for $\hat a_i$, and the regret network is trained further, from its
parameters $\theta_0$, by minimizing
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\Big[\sum_{j\in
C_s}p_\theta(j\mid s)\,\bar d_{sj} +\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p_\theta(\cdot\mid
s)\,\|\,p_{\theta_0}(\cdot\mid s)\big)\Big] +\alpha\,\Delta_C(\theta)
+\gamma\,\Delta_X(\theta),
\label{eq:exit}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{S}$ is the set of labeled states and $p_\theta(\cdot\mid
s)=\mathrm{sm}(-\hat q_\theta/\tau)$ over $C_s$. The first term, the expected
measured advantage, is linear in the labels, so that their noise averages out.
The KL term keeps the policy close to the original one, and $\Delta_C$ and
$\Delta_X$, the mean squared differences between $\hat q_\theta$ and $\hat
q_{\theta_0}$ on the candidates and on 200{,}000 state--action pairs of the
imitation data, anchor the model where no label is available. We use
$\tau=0.25$\,h, $\beta=\alpha=0.1$ and $\gamma=1$, and keep the epoch with the
largest measured gain on held-out training routes. The next round collects
labels with the new policy, and the original network is trained on all
labels.

\section{Computational Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsubsection{Instances.}
We use the 900 routes of~\cite{pagnier2026scheduling}, which combine three
route lengths (800 to 4{,}000\,km), three numbers of customers (1 to 15) and
four time-window widths, with 25 routes each, one travel-time realization per
route, and 350\,kW CS spaced on average 60\,km apart, as mandated for the
TEN-T core network by 2030~\cite{afir}. Seeds 1--19 are used for training
(639 routes solved by the LA, 56{,}104 decisions), seeds 20--21 for
validation (66 routes) and seeds 22--25 for testing (125 routes). Expert
iteration uses two fresh draws per training route and round, and holds out
seeds 17--19 to select the epoch. Finally, on routes with CS of different
powers, each power is drawn from $\{150,350,700,1000\}$\,kW, on half of them
together with the spacing, and the LA was run on 238 training and 64 test
routes.

\subsubsection{Measures.}
As in~\cite{pagnier2026scheduling}, we report the gap to the oracle on the
realized travel times, with the window penalties and in route duration only.
Differences are given in percentage points (pp), paired by route, with $\pm$
one standard error, and training seeds are averaged per route. The oracle is
within 0.5\% of optimality on 114 of the 125 test routes. Learned policies are
also compared on 16 fresh travel-time draws per route, and each test
configuration was run once. The rollout runs on one core of a computing
cluster, and the MILPs are solved with Gurobi~12.

Table~\ref{tab:base} shows that the learned policies are within 0.7\,pp of
the LA, and within 0.25\,pp in route duration only, so that most of the
difference comes from missed time windows, which are twice as frequent as for
the LA. No run is infeasible, which is due to the safety filter: without it,
18 of the 375 runs of the trees and 38 of the 375 runs of the network breach
a limit.

With the rollout, both learned policies improve on the LA, by
$0.72\pm0.33$\,pp for the trees and $0.67\pm0.34$\,pp for the network, and on
themselves by $1.40\pm0.26$ and $1.17\pm0.19$\,pp, on 109 and 118 of the 125
routes. The rollout is triggered at 38\% of the decisions but changes the
action at only 4.7\% of them, and with the network it takes a median of
11\,s, 36\,s and 2.8\,min per short, medium and long route, against 0.6, 1.9
and 3.9\,h for the LA. It should be noted that the improvement over the LA was
smaller on the validation routes ($0.29\pm0.20$\,pp with the trees).

Expert iteration starts from the network of the first training seed, with
49{,}171 labeled states in the first round and 32{,}998 in the second. On the
validation routes under 16 fresh draws, the policy improves on the original
network by $0.24\pm0.10$\,h per route after one round and by $0.35\pm0.11$\,h
after two, and we stopped since the second round was not significantly
better ($-0.11\pm0.09$\,h). On the test routes, the network after expert
iteration is at the level of the LA ($+0.03\pm0.35$\,pp) and improves on the
original network by
$0.46\pm0.20$\,pp, which the fresh draws confirm ($0.25\pm0.05$\,h per route,
better on 88 routes and worse on 31). With the rollout, its gap to the oracle
is 1.84\%, which is $1.21\pm0.32$\,pp below the LA and $0.55\pm0.18$\,pp below
the rollout of the original network.

\subsection{Where the Improvement Comes From}
\label{sec:where}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_where.pdf}
\caption{(a) Schedules between hours 40 and 72 of a long test route with five
customers, whose gaps are closest to the averages of Table~\ref{tab:base}, on
its realized travel times. (b) Predicted and measured cost of resting now
instead of the action of the trees, at the decisive states of 70 long
training routes, with $\pm$ one standard error.}
\label{fig:where}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:where}(a) shows that on a long test route, the LA and the
network reach the third customer about 9 hours before its window opens, and
the network also serves the fourth customer early. The rollout, which changes
12 decisions on this route starting with an earlier first daily rest, places
its third rest before these customers, as the oracle does, so that it serves
all customers within their windows and arrives 0.4\,h before the LA.

This is consistent with how the learned costs, and hence the scores of the
LA, price an early rest. Figure~\ref{fig:where}(b) compares the predicted and
measured cost of resting now at up to 8 decisive states on each of 70 long
training routes. When resting now adds a daily rest to the route, both agree,
but when it keeps the number of rests, resting now is overpriced by about 4.5
to 4.8 hours. Indeed, the truncated horizon counts the driving allowance given
up within 24 hours, but not the slack that an early rest leaves for later.

Over all test routes, the rollout of the network reduces the gap in route
duration only by 0.88\,pp and the part due to window penalties by 0.29\,pp,
and almost halves the missed windows (from 111 to 62, against 57 for the
LA).

\subsection{Charging Stations of Different Powers}
\label{sec:mixed}

When the CS of a route have different powers, the policy must also choose
where to charge. On the 64 test routes of this kind, the oracle takes 89\% of
its energy at 700 and 1000\,kW stations and the LA 82\%, against 61\% for a
network trained on routes with equal powers, which is 8.07\% above the oracle
against 2.73\% for the LA, on the 61 routes with an oracle within 1\% of
optimality. Training on the LA runs of the 238 routes with mixed powers, with
features of the next CS, raises this share to 79\% and lowers the gap to
5.52\%, still $2.79\pm0.70$\,pp behind the LA. With the rollout, whose
parameters are unchanged, this network is at the level of the LA (2.71\%,
$-0.02\pm0.26$\,pp) and better than without it on 56 of the 61 routes.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper studied how policies learned from a stochastic look-ahead can
improve on it, for the charging, break and rest decisions of an electric truck
under uncertain travel times. The main findings are as follows. First,
policies trained on the action costs of the look-ahead are close to it, and
remain feasible behind a rule-based filter. Second, rolling out the
learned policy where it hesitates improves on the look-ahead without calling
a solver, at about 1\% of its computing time, through better-timed rests and
charges. Third, expert iteration brings the network alone to the level of the
look-ahead, and its rollout improves on the look-ahead by 1.2\,pp. Finally,
with CS of different powers, the rollout closes the gap of 2.8\,pp between the
learned policy and the look-ahead.

Several directions could reduce the limitations of the present work. First,
the comparison with the look-ahead relies on one realization of the travel
times per route, and expert iteration on a single training seed. Next, the
remaining missed windows are decided by rests placed a shift earlier, and
checking the stops close to a customer did not reduce them on the validation
routes. Finally, correlated travel times and uncertain queuing times would
test the rollout under richer uncertainty.
