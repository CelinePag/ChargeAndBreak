\begin{abstract}
Battery electric trucks can match diesel route durations on long-distance
routes if charging is synchronized with the breaks and rests imposed by the EU
Hours-of-Service regulation, but uncertain travel times make this
synchronization fragile. We build upon a rolling-horizon look-ahead policy
(LA), which solves a mixed-integer linear program for every candidate action
and travel-time scenario but needs over a minute per decision, and train a
neural network and gradient-boosted trees on the costs that the LA computes
for every candidate action. Where the learned policy hesitates, a rollout
simulates its best candidate actions until the destination, and expert
iteration trains the network on these simulations. On 125 test routes, the
rollout reduces the gap to a perfect-hindsight oracle from 3.05\% for the LA
to 2.38\% at about one second per decision, and to 1.84\% after expert
iteration. On new routes whose charging stations have different powers, the
rollout of a network trained on such routes is at the level of the LA.
\keywords{Expert iteration \and Rollout algorithms \and Imitation learning
\and Electric trucks \and Truck driver scheduling}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Battery electric trucks are the leading pathway to decarbonize road
freight~\cite{pagnier2026linking}, but on long-distance routes their charging
stops interact with the EU Hours-of-Service (HoS) regulation~\cite{eu561},
which imposes a 45-minute break after at most 4.5 hours of driving and a daily
rest within every 24 hours. Because such a break is long enough for a
substantial fast charge, synchronizing charging with breaks and rests keeps
electric trucks close to diesel route durations~\cite{brockmann2025}. However,
travel times are uncertain, and a delay on one leg uses up driving time and
energy before the planned stop is reached, so that the charging, break and
rest decisions have to be revised at every stop.

This paper builds upon~\cite{pagnier2026scheduling}, which formulated this
problem as a sequential decision model and compared several policies in a
discrete-event simulator. The best one is a rolling-horizon look-ahead (LA),
which solves a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) over the next 24 hours for
every candidate action and 25 travel-time scenarios. It stays within 2 to 5\%
of a perfect-hindsight oracle, but takes 77 seconds per decision on average
and up to 18 minutes, and only looks 24 hours ahead. A policy learned from the
LA decides in milliseconds, and can therefore be simulated until the
destination. This motivates our research questions: Can a policy learned from
the LA be used to improve on the LA? And can this improvement be learned back
into a fast policy?

Our contributions are as follows. First, we train gradient-boosted trees and a
neural network on the costs that the LA computes for all candidate actions,
behind a rule-based safety filter. Second, we propose a decisive-state
rollout, which simulates the best candidate actions of the learned policy
where it hesitates, without calling a solver. Third, we train the network on
the evaluations of this rollout, in the manner of expert
iteration~\cite{anthony2017}. Finally, we evaluate these policies on new
routes whose charging stations (CS) have different powers, with and without
training on such routes. The rollout improves on the LA at about 1\% of its
decision time, and after expert iteration the network alone matches the LA.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Sections~\ref{sec:related}
and~\ref{sec:problem} review related work and the problem,
Sect.~\ref{sec:method} presents the methods, Sect.~\ref{sec:exp} reports the
results, and Sect.~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsubsection{Imitation learning.}
Fast policies for sequential decision problems are often obtained by imitating
an optimization expert. AggreVaTe~\cite{ross_bagnell2014,sun2017aggrevated}
learns the expert's cost of every action, which bounds the cost of the learned
policy by that of the expert plus the learning error, and policies have also
been learned from hindsight-optimal solutions~\cite{baty2024,sinclair2023}.
Learned controllers are often executed behind a safety
filter~\cite{wabersich2021} or a shield~\cite{alshiekh2018}.

\subsubsection{Rollout.}
Rollout algorithms improve a base policy by simulating it after each candidate
action~\cite{tesauro1996,bertsekas1997}, which is one step of policy
iteration, and they are widely used in stochastic vehicle
routing~\cite{goodson2017,kullman2021}. Expert iteration~\cite{anthony2017}
trains a neural network on the decisions of a search that improves on it, and
Chang et al.~\cite{chang2015lols} argue that improving on an expert requires
roll-outs with the learned policy.

\subsubsection{Electric trucks and driving-time regulations.}
Truck driver scheduling under EU rules~\cite{goel2010,garaix2024} and electric
vehicle routing with recharging stations~\cite{schneider2014,montoya2017} have
mostly been studied separately, and the works that combine them assume
deterministic travel times~\cite{bai2023,brockmann2025}.

\subsubsection{Contribution.}
To our knowledge, learned policies have not yet been applied to the joint
charging and rest decisions of electric trucks, nor used as the base policy of
a rollout that improves on the look-ahead they are learned from. This matters
in practice, since the LA is the best policy of~\cite{pagnier2026scheduling}
but needs a MILP solver and up to 18 minutes for a decision. The policies of
this paper decide better than the LA, in about a second and without a solver,
so that they can run on board and update the schedule of the driver in real
time, at every stop and after every delay.

\section{Problem Description}
\label{sec:problem}

We recall the sequential decision model of~\cite{pagnier2026scheduling},
which follows the framework of Powell~\cite{powell2019}, and refer
to~\cite{pagnier2026scheduling} for the MILP formulation and the values of all
parameters.

A truck drives a fixed route of stops $i\in\mathcal{I}=\{0,\dots,N\}$:
customers $\mathcal{C}$, CS $\mathcal{K}$ and rest areas $\mathcal{L}$. Leg $i$
has an average travel time $D_i$ and a realized travel time $D_i\,\xi_i$,
where $\xi_i=\min\{\underline{\xi}+Y_i,\ \overline{\xi}\}$, $Y_i$ is lognormal
and independent across legs, and $\mathbb{E}[\xi_i]=1$. The energy of a leg
follows from its realized speed, and each customer has a service time and a
soft time window. The state $\Gamma_i$ contains the clock time, the battery
energy and the counters of the HoS regulation. An action $a_i\in\mathcal{A}_i$
combines a charging decision (charge or not), a break (none, 45 minutes, or
the 15 and 30 minutes of a split break) and a daily rest (none, 11 or 9
hours). Breaks and rests have fixed durations, and the charging duration is
set once the action is chosen. A driver may drive at most 4.5 hours without a
break and 9 hours per shift (10 hours twice a week), and must complete a daily
rest within 24 hours of the previous one~\cite{eu561,eu2002_15}, and a charge
counts as a break if it lasts as long. The battery must stay above a minimum
energy, and charging slows down above a threshold. A run in which the truck is
stranded or the driver breaches a limit is infeasible. A policy $\pi$ chooses
the action $a_i=A^\pi(\Gamma_i)$ from the state, and we seek one that never
makes a run infeasible and minimizes
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\Big[u^t_N+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}\beta_i\delta_i\Big],
\label{eq:obj}
\end{equation}
the expected arrival time at the destination over the travel times, plus a
penalty $\beta_i$ for each customer served outside its window ($\delta_i=1$).

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:method}

\label{fig:methods}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:methods} summarizes the policies. The LA and the rollout both
look ahead, but the LA optimizes the next 24 hours of each scenario with its
travel times known, whereas the rollout simulates a learned policy, which does
not know them, until the destination.

\subsection{Methods of~\cite{pagnier2026scheduling}}

The simulator executes the action of a policy at each stop, reveals the
realized travel time and energy of the next leg, and updates the state. The
greedy heuristic mimics a driver without planning tools, who stops only when
forced to and charges to full at every stop. The LA draws $S=25$ travel-time
scenarios and solves, for every $a\in\mathcal{A}_i$ and every scenario $k$,
the MILP of~\cite{pagnier2026scheduling} up to a horizon stop $h$ within 24
hours, with $a$ fixed and the travel times of scenario $k$ known. It executes
the action with the lowest score $f_i(a)=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{k}z_i(a,\xi^{(k)})$,
where $z_i(a,\xi^{(k)})$ is the optimal arrival time at $h$ plus penalties,
and takes its charging duration from a final MILP at average travel times.
Finally, the oracle solves the MILP over the whole route once the run is
finished, with the realized travel times known, which gives the best schedule
in hindsight. It is a benchmark rather than a policy, and we report the gap of
each policy to it.

\subsection{Learned Policies}

\subsubsection{Training data.}
At every stop of the 639 training routes that the LA solved
in~\cite{pagnier2026scheduling}, the LA computes the score $f_i(a)$ of every
action, and we record all of them (317{,}802 state--action pairs from 56{,}104
decisions). The regret of an action is how much its score exceeds the best
score at the same stop, $q_i(a)=f_i(a)-\min_{a'\in\mathcal{A}^F_i}f_i(a')$,
over the actions $\mathcal{A}^F_i$ that are feasible in all scenarios. The
best action thus has a regret of 0, and what all actions of a stop have in
common, such as the remaining route, is removed.

\subsubsection{Features.}
Each state--action pair is described by 95 features computed at average travel
times. The 77 state features describe how close the truck is to each limit and
what lies ahead, namely the slack to each regulatory limit, the energy margins
to the next three CS, the next two customers and the slack to their windows, a
summary of the next 24 hours, and what remains of the route. The 18 action
features describe the charge, break and rest of the action, its duration, and
the window slack after it. In preliminary experiments, smaller sets, and a
larger one that adds the next 20 stops one by one, performed worse.

\subsubsection{Models.}
A learned policy uses three learned models. The regret model predicts the
regret of every action of the stop. The feasibility model predicts whether an
action was feasible in all scenarios of the LA, so that the policy avoids the
actions that the LA would reject. The charging model predicts, for an action
that charges, how long the LA would charge, since the action itself only
states whether to charge. The three models are trained once as gradient-boosted
trees (LightGBM~\cite{ke2017}, up to 1{,}200 trees of 63 leaves and depth 6,
learning rate 0.12), which gives the trees policy, and once as neural networks
(PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019}, one multilayer perceptron per model with SiLU
activations, hidden layers of 256, 256 and 128 neurons for the regret and of
128 and 128 for the others, AdamW with a learning rate of $10^{-3}$), which
gives the network policy. The regret is fitted with a Huber loss, quadratic
for errors below 1 hour and linear above, so that the few regrets of 10 hours
or more do not dominate the fit. Since only the best action is executed, the
network also minimizes the cross-entropy between $\mathrm{sm}(-q_i/\tau)$ and
$\mathrm{sm}(-\hat q_i/\tau)$, the probabilities that each action of the stop
is the best one under the true and the predicted regrets (softmax over
$\mathcal{A}^F_i$, $\tau=0.25$\,h), which penalizes errors in the ranking of
the actions. Training stops when the loss on the validation routes no longer
improves.

\subsubsection{Safety filter.}
The learned policy $\hat\pi$ picks the action with the lowest predicted regret
$\hat q_i(a)$ among those predicted feasible, $\hat p_i(a)\ge0.5$, and allowed
by a rule-based filter $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_i\subseteq\mathcal{A}_i$, which is
not learned. The filter removes an action if a counter would exceed its limit
when the next leg is driven at the 0.99 quantile of $\xi_i$, if the stop would
not fit before the next daily rest, or if another action is mandatory, and the
charge is clipped between the energy needed to reach the next CS at that
quantile and a full battery.

\subsection{Decisive-State Rollout}

The rollout relies on a simple property~\cite{bertsekas1997}. If, at a stop, a
few candidate actions are compared by simulating the rest of the route with
$\hat\pi$ taking all later decisions, and the best one is applied, the
resulting policy is on average no worse than $\hat\pi$, as long as the action
of $\hat\pi$ is among the candidates. We make this comparison only at decisive
stops, where the best rest and the best action without rest are within 2 hours
of each other in predicted regret, or the two best actions within 0.25 hour,
since elsewhere one action is clearly better and simulating would rarely
change the decision. The four candidates have the lowest predicted regrets,
always including the best rest and the best action without rest, and each one
is simulated until the destination under the same 32 travel-time futures, so
that they are compared under the same luck~\cite{ng2000pegasus}. The futures
are simulated in rounds of 8, after which a candidate whose average extra cost
over the action of $\hat\pi$ exceeds two standard errors is dropped, and the
rollout applies the candidate with the lowest average cost. All candidates pass
the safety filter, and all simulated decisions are taken by $\hat\pi$. These
parameters were fixed before any validation run.

\subsection{Expert Iteration}

The rollout needs a simulator and up to about 30\,s at a decisive stop. Expert
iteration~\cite{anthony2017} trains the network policy to make the choices of
its own rollout, in rounds. In each round, the network drives the 639 training
routes under two new travel-time draws, and at every decisive stop the rollout
measures, for each of its four candidate actions $j$, the hours $\bar d_j$ that
it saves or costs compared with the action of the network. The action of the
network is executed, however, so that the labeled stops are those that the
network reaches by itself, as in DAgger~\cite{ross2011}. The regret model of
the network is then trained further on the labeled stops of all rounds, from
its original parameters $\theta_0$, by minimizing
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{j\in
C_s}p_\theta(j\mid s)\,\bar d_{sj}+R(\theta),
\label{eq:exit}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}$ is the set of labeled stops, $C_s$ the candidate actions
compared by the rollout at stop $s$, and $p_\theta(j\mid s)$, the softmax of
$-\hat q_\theta/\tau$ over $C_s$, the probability that the network picks $j$.
The first term, the expected hours that the choice of the network costs
compared with its original action, decreases when the network moves towards
the candidates that the rollout found better. The term $R(\theta)$ keeps the
network close to the original one, through the Kullback--Leibler divergence
between $p_\theta$ and $p_{\theta_0}$ and the squared change of the predicted
regrets on the candidates and on 200{,}000 training pairs, and the epoch is
selected on 3 held-out training routes of each instance class.

\section{Computational Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Instances and Measures}
\label{sec:setup}

The 900 routes of~\cite{pagnier2026scheduling} belong to 36 instance classes,
which combine three ranges of route length (800 to 4{,}000\,km), three ranges
of number of customers (1 to 15) and four types of time windows, with 25 routes
per class. Travel times have a coefficient of variation of 0.15, the battery
has 500\,kWh, and the 350\,kW CS are 60\,km apart on average, as mandated by
2030~\cite{afir}. Of the 25 routes of each instance class, 19 are used for
training, 2 for validation and 4 for testing, of which 125 test routes have an
LA run. As in~\cite{pagnier2026scheduling}, we report the gap to the oracle on
the realized travel times, with a window penalty $\beta_i$ of 30 minutes and in
route duration only, and differences in percentage points (pp), paired by
route, $\pm$ one standard error. Since training is random, the trees and the
network are each trained three times on the same routes, and their results are
averaged. Each test route has one stored realization of the travel times, on
which the LA and the oracle were run. To compare the learned policies with
each other more precisely, we also simulate them on 16 other realizations per
test route, drawn from the same distribution.

Table~\ref{tab:base} shows that the learned policies are within 0.7\,pp of
the LA, and within 0.25\,pp in route duration only, so that most of the
difference comes from missed time windows. No run is infeasible, which is due
to the safety filter, since without it 18 of the 375 runs of the trees and 38
of the 375 runs of the network breach a limit.

With the rollout, both learned policies improve on the LA, by
$0.72\pm0.33$\,pp for the trees and $0.67\pm0.34$\,pp for the network, and on
themselves on 109 and 118 of the 125 routes. The rollout is triggered at 38\%
of the stops and changes the action at 4.7\% of them, and with the network it
takes about 1\,s per stop on average and at most about 30\,s, against 77\,s and
18\,min for the LA.

Expert iteration was applied to the first of the three trained networks. After
two rounds (49{,}171 and 32{,}998 labeled stops), this network alone is at the
level of the LA ($+0.03\pm0.35$\,pp) and improves on its original version by
$0.46\pm0.20$\,pp, which the 16 other realizations per route confirm
($0.25\pm0.05$\,h per route, better on 88 routes and worse on 31), and its
rollout is $1.21\pm0.32$\,pp below the LA. A third round was not run, since
the second improved little on the first on the validation routes.

\subsubsection{Where the improvement comes from.}
Figure~\ref{fig:schedule} shows that on a long test route, the LA and the
network reach the third customer about 9 hours before its window opens, and
the network also serves the fourth customer early. The rollout changes 12
decisions on this route, starting with an earlier first daily rest, so that
its third rest falls before these customers, as for the oracle, and it serves
all customers within their windows. More generally, at the decisive stops of
70 long training routes, resting now without adding a daily rest to the trip
is estimated by the trees, which learn from the LA, at 5.2 hours, but costs
only 1.6 hours when simulated until the destination, since the LA only looks
24 hours ahead.

Finally, we create new routes, which were not studied
in~\cite{pagnier2026scheduling}, in which the power of each CS is drawn from
$\{150,350,700,1000\}$\,kW, on half of them together with the spacing between
CS, so that the policy must also choose where to charge. The LA was run on 238
training and 64 test routes of this kind. We compare two networks. The first
is trained on the base case and on the eight one-at-a-time variants
of~\cite{pagnier2026scheduling}, in which one parameter changes for the whole
route (a battery of 300 to 900\,kWh, CS of 150 to 1000\,kW, or CS 30 or 100\,km
apart), so that it has seen every power but never two powers on the same route.
The second is also trained on the 238 routes with mixed powers. Both networks
also read, for the CS at the stop and for each of the next six, its power,
distance and charging time. Table~\ref{tab:other} shows that the network that never saw two powers on a route takes 18\% of its energy at 150\,kW CS, against 1\% for the oracle, and is 8.07\% above the oracle. The rollout moves most of this energy to faster CS and lowers the gap to 4.99\%, which is still $2.26\pm0.63$\,pp behind the LA. Trained on routes with mixed powers, the network charges almost where the LA does, and with the rollout it takes 85\% of its energy at 700 and 1000\,kW, close to the 89\% of the oracle, and is at the level of the LA (2.45\%, $-0.28\pm0.24$\,pp). The greedy heuristic, which charges to full at every stop, spreads its energy evenly over the four powers.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper studied how policies learned from a stochastic look-ahead can
improve on it, for the charging, break and rest decisions of an electric truck
under uncertain travel times. The main findings are as follows. First,
gradient-boosted trees and neural networks trained on the action costs of the
look-ahead are close to it. They perform alike, but the network decides ten
times faster and can be trained by expert iteration. Second, rolling out the
learned policy where it hesitates improves on the look-ahead at about 1\% of
its decision time, mainly by resting earlier where the look-ahead, which only
looks 24 hours ahead, overestimates the cost of an early rest. Third, expert
iteration brings the network alone to the level of the look-ahead. Finally, on
routes with CS of different powers, the rollout of a network trained on such
routes is at the level of the look-ahead. A network that never saw two powers on the same route charges too often at slow CS, which the rollout only partly corrects.

Several directions could reduce the limitations of the present work. First,
the comparison with the look-ahead relies on one realization of the travel
times per route, and expert iteration was applied to one trained network and
to the base case only. Finally, correlated travel times and uncertain queuing
times would test the rollout under richer uncertainty.
