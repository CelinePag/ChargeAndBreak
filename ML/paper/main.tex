\begin{abstract}
Battery electric trucks are competitive on long-haul routes only if they
charge during the breaks and rests imposed by the EU driving-time regulation,
which uncertain travel times make difficult. A rolling-horizon look-ahead
policy, which solves a mixed-integer linear program for every candidate action
and travel-time scenario, stays close to a perfect-hindsight benchmark but
needs more than a minute of solver time at every stop. We study when calling
this solver is worth it. First, we train gradient-boosted trees and a neural
network on the costs that the look-ahead computes for every candidate action,
behind a rule-based safety filter. On 125 test routes, these policies are
within 0.7 percentage points of the look-ahead and none of their 750 runs
breaches the regulation, whereas imitating hindsight-optimal schedules causes
unnecessary daily rests. Second, a learned gate selects the decisions passed
to the look-ahead, and a rollout check keeps its action only if it remains
better when the learned policy drives on. This hybrid policy matches the
look-ahead while calling it at 8.5\% of the decisions. \tbd{One sentence on
charging stations of different powers.}

Electric trucks \and Truck driver scheduling}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Battery electric trucks are the leading pathway to decarbonize road
freight~\cite{pagnier2026linking}, but on long-haul routes their charging
stops interact with the rules on driving time. The EU hours-of-service (HoS)
regulation~\cite{eu561} imposes a 45-minute break after at most 4.5 hours of
driving and a daily rest within every 24 hours. Since such a break is long
enough for a substantial fast charge, synchronizing charging with breaks and
rests is what keeps electric trucks close to the route durations of diesel
trucks~\cite{brockmann2025}. However, this synchronization is fragile under
uncertain travel times, since a delay on a single leg uses driving time and
energy before the planned stop is reached.

In a companion paper~\cite{pagnier_companion}\footnote{\tbd{Venue of the
companion paper, as the call for papers requires.}}, we formulated this
problem as a sequential decision model and compared several scheduling
policies in a discrete-event simulator. The best one is a rolling-horizon
look-ahead (LA), which solves a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) over the
next 24 hours for every candidate action and 25 travel-time scenarios. It
stays within 2 to 5\% of a perfect-hindsight oracle, but a decision requires
more than a minute of solver time, at every stop of every route. A learned
policy decides in milliseconds, but it offers no guarantee with respect to the
regulation, and it can make rare but costly errors. Indeed, a single wrong
decision can force an additional daily rest, which adds at least 9 hours to
the route. This motivates our research questions: Can a policy learned from
the LA take its decisions at a fraction of its cost? And at which decisions is
calling the LA still worth it?

Our contributions are as follows. First, we train a policy on the costs that
the LA computes for all candidate actions, rather than on its choice only, and
execute it behind a rule-based safety filter. We also compare this teacher
with the oracle. Second, we propose a hybrid policy in which a gate, trained on
out-of-fold errors of the learned policy, decides at which stops to call the
LA, and a rollout check keeps the action of the LA only if it remains better
when the learned policy takes the subsequent decisions. Finally, we evaluate
both policies on routes whose charging stations (CS) have different powers,
where the policy must also choose where to charge.

Our results show that the learned policies are within 0.7 percentage points
(pp) of the LA on 125 test routes, while imitating the oracle causes
additional daily rests. The hybrid policy matches the LA while calling it at
8.5\% of the decisions, and the rollout check is necessary for this result. On
routes with CS of different powers, the learned policy charges at the fast
stations almost as often as the LA, but remains 2.8\,pp behind it. \tbd{Hybrid
result on these routes.}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Our work combines three research areas, which we review in turn before
positioning our contribution.

\subsubsection{Learning from optimization experts.}
Imitation learning trains a policy on states labeled by an expert, and
DAgger~\cite{ross2011} collects these states with the learned policy.
AggreVaTe~\cite{ross_bagnell2014,sun2017aggrevated} uses the expert's
cost-to-go of every action, which bounds the cost of the learned policy by
that of the expert plus the regression error, and our training targets are of
this kind. As noted by Chang et al.~\cite{chang2015lols}, improving on the
expert requires roll-outs with the learned policy. Since each scenario is
solved with perfect information, the LA is an instance of the expectation
algorithm~\cite{vanhentenryck2006} and of hindsight
optimization~\cite{yoon2008}. Policies can also be learned from
hindsight-optimal solutions directly~\cite{baty2024,sinclair2023}. At CPAIOR,
Bourgeat et al.~\cite{bourgeat2026} imitate a global solver before fine-tuning
by reinforcement learning, and Anand et al.~\cite{anand2026} fine-tune a
MILP-based policy by reinforcement learning.

\subsubsection{Approximate predictive control and safety.}
The LA is a stochastic model predictive controller. Learned approximations of
such controllers~\cite{hertneck2018,karg2020} are often combined with a safety
filter~\cite{wabersich2021} or a shield~\cite{alshiekh2018} that restricts
unsafe actions, and Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2019certificate} learn an online
estimate of their suboptimality. Choosing between a cheap and an expensive
method is related to algorithm selection, which Guigal et
al.~\cite{guigal2026} apply per instance within a rolling horizon.

\subsubsection{Electric trucks and driving-time regulations.}
Truck driver scheduling under EU rules~\cite{goel2010,garaix2024} and electric
vehicle routing with recharging stations~\cite{schneider2014,montoya2017} have
mostly been studied separately. Recent works combine them on a fixed route
with deterministic travel times~\cite{bai2023,brockmann2025}, and Bai et
al.~\cite{bai2023} use a rollout algorithm. Dynamic variants of electric
vehicle routing are addressed with rollout policies~\cite{kullman2021} or safe
reinforcement learning~\cite{basso2022}, but without driving-time rules.

\subsubsection{Contribution.}
Table~\ref{tab:positioning} positions the present work against the most
closely related papers. In contrast with them, we learn from the action costs
of a stochastic look-ahead, and we learn at which decisions this look-ahead is
worth calling. To our knowledge, learned policies have not yet been applied to
the joint charging and rest decisions of electric trucks.

\section{Problem and Look-Ahead Policy}
\label{sec:problem}

We briefly recall the sequential decision model of the companion
paper~\cite{pagnier_companion}, which follows the framework of
Powell~\cite{powell2019}.

\subsubsection{Route and uncertainty.}
A truck drives a fixed route of stops $i\in\mathcal{I}=\{0,\dots,N\}$:
customers $\mathcal{C}$, CS $\mathcal{K}$ and rest areas $\mathcal{L}$. Leg $i$
has an average travel time $D_i$ and a realized travel time
\begin{equation}
\tilde D_i=D_i\,\xi_i,\qquad
\xi_i=\min\{\underline{\xi}+Y_i,\ \overline{\xi}\},
\label{eq:xi}
\end{equation}
where $Y_i$ is lognormal and independent across legs, with
$\mathbb{E}[\xi_i]=1$ and a coefficient of variation of 0.15,
$\underline{\xi}\approx0.889$ and $\overline{\xi}=1.6$. The energy of a leg
follows from the realized speed, and both are revealed once the leg is
driven. Each customer has a service time and a soft time window, and serving
it outside its window costs a penalty $\beta_i$ of 30 minutes.

\subsubsection{State, actions and objective.}
Upon arrival at stop $i$, the state $\Gamma_i$ contains the clock time, the
battery energy, the driving time since the last break, the driving and
working times of the shift, the time since the last daily rest, the
split-break flag, and the numbers of reduced rests and extended shifts used.
An action $a_i$ combines a charging decision (only at a CS), a break type
(45 minutes, or 15 and 30 minutes for a split break) and a rest type (11 or
9 hours), with their durations; $\mathcal{A}_i$ denotes the discrete actions
that are structurally feasible at stop $i$. A driver may not drive more than
4.5 hours without a break, nor more than 9 hours per shift (10 hours twice a
week), and must complete a daily rest within 24 hours of the previous
one~\cite{eu561,eu2002_15}, so that a shift lasts at most 13 hours (15 before a
reduced rest). A charge counts as a break only if it lasts as long as the
break. The 500\,kWh battery must stay above 20\% of its capacity, and
charging slows down above 80\%. A run in which the truck is stranded or the
driver breaches a limit is infeasible. A policy $\pi$ maps the state to an
action, $a_i=A^\pi(\Gamma_i)$, and we seek one that minimizes
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\Big[u^t_N+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}\beta_i\delta_i\Big],
\label{eq:obj}
\end{equation}
the expected arrival time at the destination plus the penalties, where
$\delta_i=1$ if customer $i$ is served outside its window.

\subsubsection{Look-ahead policy and oracle.}
At stop $i$, the LA selects a horizon end-stop $h$ within 24 hours, draws
$S=25$ travel-time scenarios $\xi^{(k)}$, and solves, for every
$a\in\mathcal{A}_i$ and every $k$, the MILP of~\cite{pagnier_companion} over
$[i,h]$ with $a$ fixed at stop $i$. The score of action $a$ is
\begin{equation}
f_i(a)=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{k=1}^{S}z_i\big(a,\xi^{(k)}\big),
\label{eq:la}
\end{equation}
where $z_i(a,\xi^{(k)})$ is the optimal arrival time at $h$ plus penalties,
or a large constant if the scenario is infeasible. The LA executes
$a^\ast\in\arg\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}_i}f_i(a)$, with durations from a final
MILP at average travel times. The oracle solves the MILP over the whole route
with the realized travel times once the run is finished. It is a benchmark
rather than a policy, and we report the gap of each policy to it.

\section{Learned and Hybrid Policies}
\label{sec:method}

\subsubsection{Learning from the action costs of the LA.}
To select its action, the LA computes $f_i(a)$ for every
$a\in\mathcal{A}_i$, and we record all these scores rather than its choice
only. Let $\mathcal{A}^F_i$ be the actions that are feasible in all
scenarios. For $a\in\mathcal{A}^F_i$, the regression target is the regret
$q_i(a)=f_i(a)-\min_{a'\in\mathcal{A}^F_i}f_i(a')$, and
$\mathbf{1}\{a\in\mathcal{A}^F_i\}$ is the target of a feasibility model. A
state--action pair is described by 95 features computed at average travel
times: the slack to each regulatory limit, the energy margin to the next
stations, the next CS and customers, and the action. We train a regret model
$\hat q$, a feasibility model $\hat p$ and a charging-duration model, either
as gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM~\cite{ke2017}) with a Huber loss for the
regret, or as multilayer perceptrons (PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019}) whose regret
model minimizes
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i}\Big[\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}^F_i}\ell\big(\hat q_i(a)-q_i(a)\big)
+\lambda\,\mathrm{CE}\big(\mathrm{sm}(-q_i/\tau),\,
\mathrm{sm}(-\hat q_i/\tau)\big)\Big],
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\ell$ is the Huber loss with a threshold of 1 hour, $\mathrm{sm}$ the
softmax over $\mathcal{A}^F_i$, CE the cross-entropy, $\tau=0.25$\,h and
$\lambda=1$. The second term penalizes errors in the ranking of the actions.

\subsubsection{Safety filter.}
The learned policy selects $\hat a_i\in\arg\min\{\hat q_i(a):
a\in\hat{\mathcal{A}}_i,\ \hat p_i(a)\ge0.5\}$. The set
$\hat{\mathcal{A}}_i\subseteq\mathcal{A}_i$ is computed by rules and is never
learned: it removes an action if a regulatory counter would exceed its limit
when the next leg is driven at the 0.99 quantile of $\xi_i$, if the stop would
not fit before the next daily rest, or if the regulation makes another action
mandatory. The charge is clipped between the energy needed to reach the next
CS at the same quantile and a full battery.

\subsubsection{A gate for calls to the LA.}
The hybrid policy follows the learned policy and calls the LA at selected
stops. In a state visited by the LA, a call is worth $v_i=q_i(\hat a_i)$, the
regret of the learned action. To obtain the errors made on unseen routes, we
split the training routes into five folds and compute $\hat a_i$ with a model
trained on the other four. A gradient-boosted regressor $\hat v$ then predicts
$\log(1+v_i)$ from signals available online: the gap between the two best
predicted costs, the gap between the best rest and the best action without
rest, the entropy of $\mathrm{sm}(-\hat q_i/\tau)$, the predicted
feasibility, the disagreement with a second learned policy, and the state
features. For a budget of $b\%$ of the decisions, the LA is called when $\hat
v$ exceeds the corresponding quantile on the validation decisions.

\subsubsection{Rollout check.}
The score~(\ref{eq:la}) assumes that the LA also takes the subsequent
decisions. In the hybrid policy, the learned policy does, and an action that
suits the plan of the LA, such as an early daily rest, can be harmful when the
learned policy drives on. When the LA returns an action
$a^{\mathrm{LA}}\neq\hat a_i$, we therefore draw $F$ travel-time futures,
apply either action, and let the learned policy drive each copy to the
destination. Let $d_k$ be the cost of the route after $\hat a_i$ minus its
cost after $a^{\mathrm{LA}}$ in future $k$. The action of the LA is kept only
if $\bar d-z\,\mathrm{se}(d)>0$, and both actions share each future so that
the comparison is paired. This is the roll-out with the learner
of~\cite{chang2015lols}, used as an acceptance test rather than for training.
Furthermore, the action of the LA must belong to $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_i$, and its
charge is raised to the safe minimum if needed. Algorithm~\ref{alg:hybrid}
summarizes one decision.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{One decision of the hybrid policy at stop $i$}
\label{alg:hybrid}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require state $\Gamma_i$, learned policy, gate $\hat v$ with threshold $\theta_b$, futures $F$, $z$
\State $a\gets\hat a_i$ \Comment{argmin of $\hat q_i$ over the filtered actions $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_i$}
\If{$|\hat{\mathcal{A}}_i|\ge2$ \textbf{and} $\hat v(\Gamma_i)\ge\theta_b$}
  \State $a^{\mathrm{LA}}\gets$ action executed by the LA in state $\Gamma_i$
  \If{$a^{\mathrm{LA}}\in\hat{\mathcal{A}}_i$ \textbf{and} $a^{\mathrm{LA}}\neq a$}
    \State draw futures $\omega_1,\dots,\omega_F$; in each, drive the learned policy to the destination after $a$ and after $a^{\mathrm{LA}}$
    \State $d_k\gets$ cost after $a$ minus cost after $a^{\mathrm{LA}}$ under $\omega_k$
    \If{$\bar d-z\,\mathrm{se}(d)>0$} $a\gets a^{\mathrm{LA}}$ \EndIf
  \EndIf
\EndIf
\State \Return $a$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Computational Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

The experiments consist of three parts. First, we compare the learned
policies with the LA. Second, we evaluate the hybrid policy, together with
ablations of the gate and of the rollout check. Finally, we evaluate the
policies on routes whose CS have different powers.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsubsection{Instances.}
We use the 900 routes of the companion paper~\cite{pagnier_companion}, which
combine three route lengths (800 to 4{,}000\,km), three numbers of customers
(1 to 15) and four time-window widths, with 25 routes and one travel-time
realization each. CS have 350\,kW and are placed on average every 60\,km, as
mandated for the TEN-T core network by 2030~\cite{afir}. Routes with seeds
1--19 are used for training (639 routes solved by the LA, 56{,}104
decisions), seeds 20--21 for validation (66 routes), and seeds 22--25 for
testing (125 routes with an LA run). For the third part, the power of each CS
is drawn from $\{150,350,700,1000\}$\,kW, either alone or together with the
spacing between stations, and the LA was run on 238 training and 64 test
routes.

\subsubsection{Measures and protocol.}
As in the companion paper, we report the gap to the oracle in route duration,
with and without the out-of-window penalty of~(\ref{eq:obj}). Differences
between policies are given in pp, paired by route, with $\pm$ one standard
error; training seeds are averaged per route first. The oracle is solved to a
0.5\% optimality gap on 114 of the 125 test routes. All design choices were
made on the validation routes, and each test configuration was run once. The
MILPs are solved with Gurobi~12.

Table~\ref{tab:learned} shows that both learned policies are within 0.7\,pp of
the LA, and within 0.25\,pp in route duration only. Indeed, most of the
difference comes from missed time windows, which are twice as frequent as for
the LA. A decision takes 1 to 5\,ms. Furthermore, no run is infeasible, which
is due to the safety filter rather than to the models: without the filter, 18
of the 375 runs of the trees and 38 of the 375 runs of the network breach a
limit.

We then train the same models to reproduce the actions of the oracle on its
stored schedules. We observe that the resulting policies are worse, by
$+2.05\pm0.48$\,pp for the trees and $+6.42\pm0.66$\,pp for the network. They
take one more daily rest than the LA on 13\% (trees) and 32\% (network) of the
runs, against 3\% and 2\% when trained on the costs of the LA. This is
explained by the information the oracle has: knowing the travel times, it
plans the last shifts close to the limits, and a policy that reproduces these
choices without this knowledge is regularly forced into an additional rest.

\subsection{Calling the Look-Ahead}
\label{sec:hybrid}

We study the hybrid policy with the trees, which leave more room for
improvement than the network on the validation routes. On the validation
decisions, the gate recovers 65\% of the regret of the learned policy when
calling the LA at 5\% of the decisions, and 87\% at 10\%, against 26\% and
30\% when calling where the best rest and the best action without rest are
closest. However, in closed loop, the gate alone helped less than these
numbers suggest. On 2 of the 66 validation routes, a call led to an additional
daily rest, adding 16 and 30\,pp to these routes. On the second one, the LA
chose an early daily rest, and the learned policy, which took the subsequent
decisions, then reached the 15-hour limit and had to rest a second time. The
rollout check was introduced for this reason. With $F=32$ futures and $z=1$,
it costs up to 30\,s of simulation per route.

Table~\ref{tab:hybrid} reports the results on the test routes. The hybrid
policy with gate and rollout check is at the level of the LA
($-0.36\pm0.33$\,pp) and improves on the learned policy alone by
$1.09\pm0.30$\,pp, while calling the LA at 8.5\% of the decisions, or about

the LA seen on the validation routes ($-0.67\pm0.28$\,pp) is not confirmed on
the test routes, and that the gain over the learned policy is concentrated on
short and medium routes.

When the CS of a route have different powers, the policy must also choose
where to charge. Figure~\ref{fig:mixed}(a) shows that the oracle takes 89\% of
its energy at 700 and 1000\,kW stations, and the LA 82\%. A learned policy
trained on routes with equal powers takes only 61\%, since its training data
never offered such a choice, and its gap to the oracle is three times that of
the LA. Adding the runs of the LA on 238 training routes with mixed powers
raises this share to 79\% and reduces the gap from 8.2\% to 5.6\%. In
comparison, the greedy heuristic, which charges to full whenever it stops,
takes 52\% of its energy at fast stations. The learned policy therefore
charges almost where the LA does, but remains 2.8\,pp behind it, and three of
its 61 runs with a solved oracle (first training seed) take an additional
daily rest.

For these routes, the second policy of the gate is the same network trained
with another seed, and the gate is selected on held-out training routes, since
the LA was not run on the validation routes. On the 30 validation routes with
a solved oracle, calling the LA where the rest margin is below 20 minutes
improves on the learned policy by $0.45\pm0.25$\,pp with the rollout check,
and is worse without it, due to one additional daily rest. \tbd{Gate with

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper studied learned policies for the charging, break and rest decisions
of an electric truck under uncertain travel times, and a hybrid policy that
calls a stochastic look-ahead only where a learned gate expects a gain.

The main findings are as follows. First, policies trained on the action costs
of the look-ahead are close to it, and a rule-based filter keeps all their
runs within the regulation, whereas imitating hindsight-optimal schedules
leads to additional daily rests. Second, the hybrid policy matches the
look-ahead at about a tenth of its computing time, provided that each action
of the look-ahead is checked by rolling out the learned policy. Finally, with
charging stations of different powers, the learned policy learns where to
charge, but a gap of 2.8\,pp to the look-ahead remains. \tbd{Hybrid on these
routes.}

Several directions could address the limitations of the present work. First,
the hybrid policies were trained with a single seed on synthetic routes.
Second, the look-ahead itself still takes additional daily rests near the end
of long routes, which neither policy removes; training the learned policy
with its own roll-outs~\cite{chang2015lols} could address this. Finally,
correlated travel times and uncertain queuing times at the stations would
test whether the gate remains reliable under richer uncertainty.
