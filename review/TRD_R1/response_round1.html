Can charging legally count as a break?

Response

We thank the referee for the reference. Our model follows exactly the interpretation reported there, and we now say so and cite the Regulation (Art. 4(d)) and the reported enforcement guidance. Charging is credited as a break only when (i) a break is declared at the same stop, (ii) the charge lasts at least the declared break, and (iii) the truck is not moved during the break. When the charge is shorter than the break, the truck is first moved off the charging bay (repositioning time Mseq), the charge is counted as work, and the full break starts afterwards. Charging combined with a daily rest is always treated in this sequential way, so a charge is never credited as rest.

The alternative identified by the referee, in which the driver stays at the bay after the charge ends until the break is complete, is legal but excluded by assumption. We justify the exclusion by the use of the charging point: a truck idling at a high-power bay blocks it for other users, which is inconsistent with the waiting times we assume at shared stations. We have quantified what the exclusion costs (details in 6.2): in the hindsight-optimal schedules, a charge combined with a break is never executed in sequential mode, at any charger power; the optimiser instead lengthens the charge to cover the break, or uses the 15 + 30 min split so that each part is covered. We now specify the law completely. 4.3 Energy uncertainty is tied mechanically to speed

Referee 4.3 Energy depends only on leg-average speed; congested legs use less energy; payload, gradient, temperature, HVAC, wind and stop-and-go are absent. Pelletier et al. (2019) model energy uncertainty separately.
Response

We agree and now state this as a deliberate scope choice. The study isolates the effect of travel-time variability on the synchronisation of charging with breaks; energy varies only through the realised speed, which couples the two risks in opposite directions (slow legs threaten the driving limits, fast legs threaten the state of charge). This coupling is why the robust plan protects time and energy with opposite corners of the support.

Two of the factors listed by the referee are known before departure rather than random: the payload profile, which follows the delivery plan, and the route gradient. They would change the nominal consumption per leg rather than add within-route randomness. Our consumption curve is evaluated at 40 t gross mass throughout, which is the upper envelope for payload: unloading at customers can only reduce consumption. Temperature, auxiliary loads and wind are genuine sources of randomness that we do not model; we cite Pelletier et al. (2019) for a treatment of energy uncertainty and give the range of consumption at 80 km/h implied by the aerodynamic parameter (1.09–1.32 kWh/km for Cd between 0.36 and 0.60) as an indication of sensitivity. Our base value, 1.23 kWh/km at 80 km/h, is above the 1.03 kWh/km average reported for the eActros 600 European tour at 40 t, so the assumed consumption is on the conservative side.

4.4 The expected-value plan is biased in energy

Referee 4.4 If E[ξ] = 1, by Jensen expected speed exceeds nominal and expected energy exceeds energy at nominal speed; the plan underestimates expected energy, which can explain the SOC violations.
Response

The referee is right. With our law, the expected leg energy exceeds the energy at nominal speed by 1.4%, and the energy of a leg exceeds its nominal value with probability 0.68 (because 68% of legs are faster than nominal). The plan is built with mean travel times, not mean energies; we have renamed it the nominal plan and state the bias explicitly.

The bias explains part, not all, of the energy failures. Over a stretch between two charges of 250–400 kWh, 1.4% amounts to 3.5–5.6 kWh, whereas the median energy shortfall of the nominal plan is 8.7 kWh. The remainder comes from the variance: a plan that charges exactly enough to reach the next station with the reserve intact fails with probability close to one half at each such station even when its energy estimate is unbiased, and a route contains many such stations. The result is therefore what the referee calls mechanical, and we now present it as such (see 4.5).

We note that the look-ahead policy does not use energy at mean travel time for its committed charge: the charge decided at a stop covers the legs to the next station at the median of consumption, 3.4% above the nominal energy, which more than offsets the Jensen bias. This rule is now described in Section 5.2.

Part III — Draft abstract, highlights and keywords

Abstract (148 words)

Draft

Battery-electric trucks can match diesel trip times only if charging fits into the breaks and rests that EU drivers must take. We schedule driving, breaks, daily rests and partial charging for a battery-electric truck on a multi-day route with customer time windows, under Regulation (EC) No 561/2006, a non-linear charging curve and stochastic travel times. Four planning policies are evaluated in a discrete-event simulator on 900 instances against a hindsight benchmark. A schedule optimised for nominal travel times breaches a constraint in almost every run, mostly by small margins inside the battery reserve. A rolling-horizon look-ahead that re-plans at every stop stays on average 3.5% above the hindsight benchmark and fails in about 1% of runs. With 350 kW charge points along the corridor and no overnight charging, the electric truck needs about 10% more time than a diesel truck; with 700 kW or more, less than 5%.
